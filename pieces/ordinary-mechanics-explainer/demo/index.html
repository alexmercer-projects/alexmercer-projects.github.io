The air up there

0:32 · 162 words
TimeVoiceoverOn screen · visuals, B-roll, graphics
0:32Start with the air. Air pressure is basically the weight of all the air stacked above you. At sea level, that stack presses on you with about a hundred kilopascals, or fourteen point seven pounds on every square inch. Climb, and there’s less air on top of you, so the number drops.6Animated column of air above a figure on a beach. As the camera rises the column thins and a ticker counts down: 101 kPa → 75 → 24.
0:53At cruising altitude it’s far too thin to sit in comfortably, so airliners pressurize the cabin. But they don’t pump it all the way back to sea level. U.S. rules for airliners say that in normal flight, the cabin has to stay at a cabin altitude of no more than eight thousand feet.5Quote card, typewriter reveal: “a cabin pressure altitude of not more than 8,000 feet under normal operating conditions.” Source chip: 14 CFR 25.841.
1:15At eight thousand feet, air pressure is roughly three quarters of what it is at sea level. So on a normal flight, you go from the beach to a mountain town and back again, in a sealed tube, in one afternoon. Your body has to keep up with that. Mostly it does it without asking you.6Split screen: beach illustration left, mountain town right. Shared gauge swings 100% → 74% → 100% as a tiny plane icon crosses the frame.

The room behind your eardrum

1:39 · 159 words
TimeVoiceoverOn screen · visuals, B-roll, graphics
1:39Now the ear. Your eardrum is a thin membrane at the end of your ear canal. On the outside, there’s cabin air. On the inside, there’s a small air-filled space called the middle ear, where three small bones pass sound along to the inner ear.1Cutaway illustration builds left to right: ear canal → eardrum → middle ear. Labels pop on: hammer, anvil, stirrup.
1:58The main way air gets in or out of that space is a narrow passage called the eustachian tube. It runs from the middle ear down to the back of your nose and throat.1,7The tube draws itself in amber from the middle ear down to the back of the nose. Label: eustachian tube.
2:12Most of the time, that tube is closed. When you swallow or yawn, it opens for a moment, a little air moves through, and the pressure on both sides of your eardrum evens out. You do this all day long and never notice.2,7Loop: a swallow (simple throat icon) and the tube blinks open, a few air dots pass, a balance icon levels. Repeat 3× with a soft tick.
2:30Your eardrum moves the way it should when the pressure on both sides matches. When it doesn’t, the drum gets pushed out or pulled in, and it can’t vibrate freely. That’s the full, stuffy, underwater feeling.1,2Pattern breakEardrum membrane bows out, then in. Sound design: music bed goes muffled for two seconds, then snaps back clear.

Going up: the easy half

2:46 · 119 words
TimeVoiceoverOn screen · visuals, B-roll, graphics
2:46So, takeoff. As the plane climbs, the pressure in the cabin drops. The air trapped in your middle ear is now at a higher pressure than the cabin around it, so it pushes your eardrum outward.1,2Cabin gauge falls. Arrows inside the middle ear push the drum outward.
3:01Here’s the lucky part. When the pressure inside is higher, the tube tends to open by itself and let the extra air out. Doctors describe equalizing on the way up as passive. A click, a pop, and you barely notice it happened.4,7Re-hookTube puffs open on its own, air dots stream toward the throat. Small text: ascent = passive.
3:19It’s the same physics that puffs up a sealed bag of chips on a flight. Hold the temperature steady, lower the pressure around a gas, and it expands. That’s Boyle’s law. Your middle ear just happens to have a release valve.7B-roll: a sealed snack bag, visibly inflated, on a tray table. Lower third: Boyle’s law · P × V stays constant.

Coming down: the hard half

3:37 · 178 words
TimeVoiceoverOn screen · visuals, B-roll, graphics
3:37Landing is the reverse. As the plane descends, cabin pressure climbs back toward ground level. Now the air in your middle ear is at a lower pressure than the cabin, and your eardrum gets pulled inward.1,2Gauge rises back toward 100%. Drum bows inward; the frame takes a slight red tint.
3:52And this time the tube doesn’t open for you. It has to be opened on purpose, by swallowing, yawning, or a deliberate move we’ll get to in a second. StatPearls, a medical reference, says it plainly: equalizing is passive on the way up, but it takes an active maneuver on the way down.4Pull-quote card: “equalization … on ascent is passive, it requires an active maneuver on descent.” Source chip: StatPearls, NCBI.
4:14If you fall behind, the gap keeps growing, the tube gets harder to open, and fullness turns into pain. That’s why the advice from Mayo Clinic and ear, nose and throat doctors leans so hard on the descent. And that’s the habit I promised: sleeping through it. If you’re asleep, you’re not swallowing.1,3,4PayoffPassenger asleep against the window, neck pillow. A gauge in the corner keeps rising. Text slams in: DESCENT: STAY AWAKE.
4:35It’s also why your ears can pop in a fast elevator in a tall building, or on a winding mountain road. Any quick change in altitude does it. A plane just does it bigger, and faster.1,3Quick montage, graphics only: elevator floor counter racing upward, then a switchback road drawn as a line climbing a hill.

How to pop them properly

4:51So, the fixes. The simplest one is to swallow, and keep swallowing, especially once the descent starts. Chewing gum or sucking on hard candy helps, because both keep you swallowing.1,3Numbered list builds on the right third: 1 · Swallow. Close-up B-roll: gum unwrapped, candy in palm.

5:11If that’s not enough, there’s the Valsalva maneuver. Close your mouth, pinch your nostrils shut, and blow gently, as if you were blowing your nose. Gently is the important word. Why Your Ears Pop on Planes (and Why Landing Hurts More)

Ordinary Mechanics · not published

Why do your ears pop on a plane, and why can landing feel worse than takeoff? It comes down to cabin pressure and a tube about as wide as a pencil lead that connects your middle ear to the back of your throat. Here’s how it works, why the descent is the hard half, and what actually helps.
